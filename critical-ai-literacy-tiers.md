# Critical AI Literacy: A Tiered Framework of Capacities

*Grounded in reading-development research, academic standards, and historical-thinking scholarship, with Holocaust education as the main application.*

---

## 1. Purpose

This document turns the following conceptual statement into a sequence of **eight tiers**. Each tier describes a *state of mind* (an epistemic stance toward the text) and the *capacities* that come with it. The tiers come from a review of established literacy frameworks, so each one lines up with standards and constructs that teachers, curriculum designers, and researchers already recognize.

> Literacy involves interpreting a text's surface meaning, recognizing its references and allusions, situating it within historical and cultural contexts, and examining the conditions of its production and circulation. AI literacy similarly requires more than surface-level recognition of AI-generated content… In Holocaust education, this deeper literacy requires distinguishing the appearance of historical authenticity from an accountable relationship to historical evidence.

## 2. Literature Review

### 2.1 Reading development and "levels" of comprehension

| Source | Core construct | Relevance |
|---|---|---|
| **Chall (1983/1996)**, *Stages of Reading Development* | Six stages (0–5). The last two are Stage 4, *Multiple Viewpoints*, and Stage 5, *Construction & Reconstruction*. | The classic developmental account. It shows reading maturing from decoding into weighing perspectives and building one's own judgment. This is the same arc the conceptual statement describes. |
| **Kintsch (1988, 1998)**, Construction–Integration model | Three levels of representation: *surface code → textbase → situation model* | The psychological consensus that comprehension moves from wording, to propositions, to an integrated mental model that draws on prior knowledge. It supports Tiers 2–3. |
| **RAND Reading Study Group (2002)**, *Reading for Understanding* | Comprehension comes from the interaction of *reader, text, activity,* and *sociocultural context*. | Context and purpose belong to comprehension itself; they are not optional extras. This supports Tiers 4 and 7. |
| **Shanahan & Shanahan (2008)**, disciplinary literacy pyramid | *Basic → intermediate → disciplinary* literacy | Literacy at the highest levels is discipline-specific. Reading like a historian is a distinct, learned practice. This supports Tier 8. |
| **Perfetti, Rouet & Britt (1999)**; **Rouet & Britt (2011)**, Documents Model / MD-TRACE | *Intertext model* (source ↔ content links) alongside the *situation model* | Skilled readers of multiple documents track **who said what, and why**. This "sourcing" is weak in novices and has to be taught. It supports Tiers 4–5. |
| **Adler & Van Doren (1972)**, *How to Read a Book* | Elementary → inspectional → analytical → syntopical reading | A long-standing humanistic hierarchy that ends in comparison across many texts. |
| **Scholes (1985)**, *Textual Power* | *Reading* (within) → *interpretation* (upon) → *criticism* (against) | A literary-studies three-step that runs closely parallel to Tiers 2 → 3/6 → 7. |
| **Luke & Freebody (1990, 1999)**, Four Resources Model | *Code breaker, text participant, text user, text analyst* | Widely adopted (Australia, UK, US). The authors stress that the four roles are **concurrent rather than strictly sequential**. See the caveat in §5. |

### 2.2 Standards and large-scale assessment frameworks

| Source | Structure | Mapping notes |
|---|---|---|
| **OECD PISA Reading Framework (2018; continued 2025)** | Eight proficiency levels (1c, 1b, 1a, 2–6). Processes: *locate information → understand → evaluate & reflect* (including assessing quality, credibility, and handling conflict across sources). | Level 2 is the "baseline" (main idea, explicit information). Levels 5–6 require evaluating credibility, reflecting on purpose, and integrating conflicting multiple sources. |
| **Common Core State Standards: ELA anchor standards R.1–R.10 (NGA/CCSSO, 2010)** | *Key Ideas & Details* (R.1–3); *Craft & Structure* (R.4–6); *Integration of Knowledge & Ideas* (R.7–9); *Range* (R.10). Also RH.6-12 (Literacy in History/Social Studies). | These clusters map almost one-to-one onto Tiers 2, 3/6, and 5/7. RH.6-12.6 and RH.6-12.8–9 (point of view, evidence vs. speculation, comparing primary and secondary sources) bear directly on the Holocaust case. |
| **NAEP Reading Framework (NAGB, 2026)** | Cognitive targets: *locate/recall → integrate/interpret → critique/evaluate*. Achievement levels: Basic / Proficient / Advanced. | The US national three-step that confirms the same progression. |
| **NCSS C3 Framework (2013)**, Dimension 3 | *Evaluating Sources and Using Evidence* | The social-studies standard behind Tier 5. |
| **Webb's Depth of Knowledge (1997, 2002)** | DOK 1 Recall → 2 Skill/Concept → 3 Strategic Thinking → 4 Extended Thinking | Widely used to align tasks with standards. DOK measures **complexity, not difficulty**. |
| **Revised Bloom's Taxonomy (Anderson & Krathwohl, 2001)** | Remember → Understand → Apply → Analyze → Evaluate → Create | This is the backbone of Ng et al.'s (2021) AI-literacy model (see §2.4). |
| **SOLO Taxonomy (Biggs & Collis, 1982)** | Prestructural → Unistructural → Multistructural → Relational → Extended Abstract | Describes how *structured* a learner's understanding is. It is useful for writing rubrics. |

### 2.3 Epistemic development (the "states of mind")

| Source | Stages | Relevance |
|---|---|---|
| **Kuhn, Cheney & Weinstock (2000)**; **Kuhn (1999)** | *Realist → Absolutist → Multiplist → Evaluativist* | This is the best-supported developmental account of how people treat knowledge claims. Absolutists treat AI output as simply "true" or "fake". Multiplists treat it as "just another opinion". Evaluativists weigh it against evidence. It supplies the **"state of mind"** column. |
| **King & Kitchener (1994)**, Reflective Judgment Model | Seven stages: pre-reflective (1–3), quasi-reflective (4–5), reflective (6–7) | Reflective judgment (Stages 6–7) means justifying claims through evidence evaluated across contexts. It matches Tier 8. |
| **Perry (1970)** | Dualism → Multiplicity → Relativism → Commitment within relativism | The historical source of the Kuhn and King & Kitchener models. |
| **Greene, Sandoval & Bråten (2016)**, *Handbook of Epistemic Cognition* | Aims, ideals, and reliable processes for knowing (the AIR model, Chinn et al.) | Current consensus framing. Literacy includes knowing **how** a claim was produced and whether that process is reliable. |

### 2.4 Media, information, and AI literacy

| Source | Construct | Relevance |
|---|---|---|
| **Wineburg (2001)**; **Reisman (2012)**, *Reading Like a Historian* | *Sourcing, contextualization, corroboration, close reading* | The canonical heuristics of historical reading. Wineburg calls historical thinking an "unnatural act", one that has to be taught explicitly. |
| **McGrew, Breakstone, Ortega, Smith & Wineburg (2018)**; **Wineburg & McGrew (2019)**, Civic Online Reasoning | *Who's behind it? What's the evidence? What do other sources say?* Lateral reading | Students and even experts are poor at judging online credibility. Fact-checkers succeed by leaving the text to check its source. This is the direct model for Tier 5. |
| **ACRL Framework for Information Literacy (2015)** | Six frames, including *Authority Is Constructed and Contextual* and *Information Creation as a Process* | The higher-education standard for understanding production and circulation (Tier 4). |
| **Center for Media Literacy, Five Core Concepts (Thoman & Jolls, 2005)**; **NAMLE Core Principles (2007)**; **Hobbs (2010)** | "All media messages are constructed"; "media have embedded values and points of view"; "different people experience the same message differently" | Supports Tiers 4, 6, and 7. |
| **Long & Magerko (2020)**, "What is AI Literacy?" (CHI) | 17 competencies under five questions: *What is AI? What can AI do? How does AI work? How should AI be used? How do people perceive AI?* | The most cited definition. "How does AI work" underlies Tier 4. |
| **Ng, Leung, Chu & Qiao (2021)**, *Computers & Education: AI* | *Know & understand → Use & apply → Evaluate & create → Ethical issues*, explicitly mapped onto Bloom | A precedent for building AI literacy on established cognitive taxonomies, which is the approach taken here. |
| **UNESCO AI Competency Framework for Students (2024)** | Twelve competencies in four dimensions (Human-centred mindset, Ethics of AI, AI techniques & applications, AI system design) across *Understand → Apply → Create* | The international policy anchor. |
| **Jakesch, Hancock & Naaman (2023)**, *PNAS* | "Human heuristics for AI-generated language are flawed" | People cannot reliably detect AI text and use misleading cues to try. This is the empirical case that **recognition alone is not literacy** (Tier 1). |

### 2.5 Credibility cues: fluency, emotion, narrative

| Source | Finding | Relevance |
|---|---|---|
| **Reber & Schwarz (1999)**; **Alter & Oppenheimer (2009)** | **Processing fluency**: easy-to-process statements are judged more true. | LLM prose is optimized for fluency, which explains "familiar language creates an impression of credibility". |
| **Hasher, Goldstein & Toppino (1977)**; **Fazio et al. (2015)** | **Illusory truth effect**: repetition and familiarity raise perceived truth, even against prior knowledge. | Shows how reproduced conventions and tropes feel authentic. |
| **Green & Brock (2000)** | **Narrative transportation**: absorption in a story reduces counter-arguing. | Explains why narrative structures create credibility, which matters especially for fabricated "testimony". |
| **Slovic et al. (2007)**, affect heuristic | Emotional reactions substitute for analytic judgment. | Emotional cues are a credibility mechanism (Tier 6). |
| **Bender, Gebru, McMillan-Major & Shmitchell (2021)** | LLMs reproduce the hegemonic viewpoints in their training data and produce "coherence in the eye of the beholder". | Underlies Tiers 4 and 7: the conventions AI reproduces, and whose perspectives are missing. |

### 2.6 Critical literacy and historical consciousness

| Source | Construct | Relevance |
|---|---|---|
| **Freire (1970)**; **Freire & Macedo (1987)** | Reading "the word and the world" | The foundation of critical literacy. |
| **Lewison, Flint & Van Sluys (2002)** | Four dimensions: disrupting the commonplace; interrogating multiple viewpoints; focusing on sociopolitical issues; taking action | Operationalizes Tier 7. |
| **Janks (2010)**, *Literacy and Power* | Domination, access, diversity, design | Examines whose language and perspectives are privileged. |
| **Seixas & Morton (2013)**, *The Big Six Historical Thinking Concepts* | Significance; evidence; continuity & change; cause & consequence; historical perspectives; ethical dimension | The historical-thinking standard in Canada and internationally. *Evidence*, *perspectives*, and *ethical dimension* anchor Tiers 5, 7, and 8. |
| **Landsberg (2004)**, *Prosthetic Memory*; **Hirsch (2012)**, *The Generation of Postmemory* | Mediated, technologically produced memories of events one did not live through | The theory behind AI representations that "shape or even replace encounters with the past". |
| **IHRA (2019)**, *Recommendations for Teaching and Learning about the Holocaust*; **USHMM**, *Guidelines for Teaching About the Holocaust* | Use primary sources and testimony; contextualize; avoid simulations, simple answers, and comparisons that trivialize; individualize victims without fictionalizing them | The disciplinary standard against which AI-generated Holocaust content is judged. |
| **UNESCO & World Jewish Congress (2024)**, *AI and the Holocaust: Rewriting History?* (Makhortykh) | Generative AI risks fabricating Holocaust "facts", deepfaked evidence, oversimplification, and amplifying denial and distortion | The direct policy rationale for this framework. |

## 3. Synthesis: The Eight Tiers

**Design principles drawn from the review**

1. **Fewer than nine tiers.** Comparable instruments use 3 (NAEP), 4 (DOK, Kuhn), 5 (SOLO), 6 (Bloom, Chall), 7 (King & Kitchener), and 8 (PISA) levels. Eight tiers keeps the resolution of PISA while staying usable.
2. **Each tier is named as a state of mind** (epistemic stance, after Kuhn and King & Kitchener) **and as observable capacities** (standards language).
3. **The ordering follows the conceptual statement:** surface → references and conventions → production → evidence → presentation → perspective → historical accountability. This matches the cross-framework progression *locate → interpret → evaluate → reflect and reconstruct*.

### 3.1 Main table

| Tier | State of mind | The learner can… | Holocaust-education exemplar |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Detecting**<br>*"Is this AI?"* | **Binary / absolutist.** Provenance is treated as the whole question: content is either "real" or "fake". | Notice signs that content may be machine-generated. Use disclosure labels, metadata, and platform context. Explain why detection by eye or ear is unreliable. | Flags a "survivor quote" on social media as possibly AI-generated, but has no further way to evaluate it. |
| **2. Comprehending**<br>*"What does it say?"* | **Literal / receptive.** The text is taken on its own terms. | Accurately paraphrase and summarize the output. Identify its central claims, named people, dates, and places. Separate main claims from supporting detail. | Summarizes what a chatbot says about the Wannsee Conference, and lists its specific factual assertions so they can be checked. |
| **3. Recognizing conventions & intertexts**<br>*"What does this sound like, and what is it echoing?"* | **Interpretive / genre-aware.** Sees that the text is *made of* other texts. | Identify the genres, registers, and tropes the output reproduces (documentary narration, diary voice, testimony cadence, textbook summary). Recognize allusions and recycled phrasings. Notice clichés and composite figures. | Recognizes that a generated "diary entry" borrows the voice of Anne Frank's diary, and that a generated "liberation photo" reuses the visual grammar of well-known 1945 photographs. |
| **4. Understanding production & circulation**<br>*"How and why did this come to exist, and how did it reach me?"* | **Constructivist.** Understands that texts are *constructed* by processes, institutions, and incentives. | Explain at an appropriate level how generative models produce plausible text and images: prediction from training data, prompts, fine-tuning, and safety layers. Explain why plausibility is not the same as truth. Identify who deployed the tool, for what purpose, and how algorithms and platforms circulated it. | Explains why a model can produce a confident, specific, but nonexistent deportation record. Traces how a viral AI "Holocaust story" spread through engagement-driven feeds. |
| **5. Evaluating evidence**<br>*"What supports this, and what do other sources say?"* | **Evidentiary / emerging evaluativist.** Claims require warrant. | Separate claims that can be checked from those that cannot. Read laterally. Trace claims to primary sources, archives, and scholarship. Corroborate across independent sources. Tell evidence from speculation (RH.8). Notice when citations are missing or fabricated. | Checks a generated claim against the USHMM Holocaust Encyclopedia, Yad Vashem, the Arolsen Archives, or a USC Shoah Foundation testimony. Finds that a cited "source" does not exist. |
| **6. Analyzing rhetoric & affect**<br>*"How is this working on me?"* | **Metacognitive / reflexive.** Aware of one's own response as something to examine. | Identify how fluency, familiarity, emotional cues, and narrative arcs produce an impression of credibility. Recognize transportation and affective pull in oneself. Explain how design choices (voice, first-person framing, imagery, music) shape interpretation (R.6). | Analyzes why an AI-voiced first-person "survivor" monologue feels authentic and moving, and how that feeling stands in for evidence. |
| **7. Interrogating perspective & absence**<br>*"Whose story is this, and whose is missing, flattened, or transformed?"* | **Critical / multiperspectival.** Treats representation as a matter of power and selection. | Identify whose perspectives are represented, ventriloquized, distorted, or absent: victims, perpetrators, bystanders, rescuers, and specific groups such as Roma and Sinti, disabled people, and LGBTQ+ people. Detect training-data bias, sanitizing, redemptive arcs, and false balance. Connect these to how they could be used for distortion or denial. | Notices that a chatbot's account centers rescuers and a redemptive ending. Notices that it erases Roma victims, softens perpetrator agency, or gives "both sides" framing to denial claims. |
| **8. Exercising historical accountability**<br>*"What is my responsibility to the past, and to how it is remembered?"* | **Reflective judgment / evaluativist.** Justifies conclusions through evidence while recognizing ethical stakes. | Distinguish the *appearance* of historical authenticity from an *accountable relationship* to evidence: traceable provenance, scholarly accountability, and consent of witnesses. Judge when AI mediation helps or replaces encounters with the past (prosthetic memory). Make and defend reasoned decisions about the use, creation, and sharing of AI content about atrocity. Act on those decisions, for example by correcting distortion. | Compares an unsourced "chat with a Holocaust victim" app with testimony-based, curated projects such as USC Shoah Foundation's *Dimensions in Testimony*. Argues where the ethical line falls, consistent with IHRA guidance against simulation and fictionalization of victims. |

## 4. Condensed version (for slides or rubrics)

| # | Tier | One-line state of mind |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Detecting | "This might be AI." |
| 2 | Comprehending | "This is what it claims." |
| 3 | Recognizing conventions | "This sounds like (and borrows from) X." |
| 4 | Understanding production | "This was produced *this way*, for *these reasons*, and reached me *like this*." |
| 5 | Evaluating evidence | "This is (or isn't) supported, and here is how I know." |
| 6 | Analyzing rhetoric & affect | "This feels true *because of how it's built*, not because of what supports it." |
| 7 | Interrogating perspective | "This speaks for some people, over others, and leaves others out." |
| 8 | Historical accountability | "An authentic-*seeming* past isn't an accountable one, and I have a responsibility in how the past is remembered." |

## 5. Caveats from the literature

- **Tiers are cumulative in demand, not strictly sequential.** Luke & Freebody (1999) and the RAND Reading Study Group (2002) both warn against treating literacy practices as a ladder that must be climbed in order. A middle-schooler can do Tier 7 work on a simple text. Webb's DOK likewise measures *complexity* of thinking, not grade level. Use the tiers to design and assess tasks, not to gate students.
- **Tier 1 is necessary but weak.** Empirical work (Jakesch et al., 2023) shows human detection of AI text is unreliable. AI-text detectors are also unreliable and biased against non-native writers (Liang et al., 2023). This is the central empirical justification for the framework's premise.
- **Epistemic stance is domain-specific.** Kuhn and King & Kitchener both find that people may be evaluativist in one domain and absolutist in another. Holocaust content, given its emotional and moral weight, may pull students *back down* to absolutist or multiplist stances. This argues for treating Tier 6 explicitly.
- **Multiplism is a known hazard specific to this domain.** A student who reaches multiplist reasoning ("it's all just perspectives") without reaching evaluativist reasoning is vulnerable to denial and distortion framed as "another side". Tiers 5 and 8 should be taught together with Tier 7.
- **PISA, Chall, and SOLO mappings are approximations.** Those frameworks were built for general reading, not for multimodal or AI-mediated texts.
